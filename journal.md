## 9/14/26
I found the process of creating a website with Markdown and GitHub both engaging and confusing. Markdown itself wasn't the issue, as I started using Obsidian this summer, which relies on Markdown files, so I was already somewhat comfortable with the format. Therefore, I found myself able enough to recognize most of the notation in the template README in the version-control-workflow folder without needing the cheat sheet too often. I look forward to learning more about the concepts behind these projects and how they fit into this workflow. That said, I may be wrong in my observations, but I was surprised to notice what I perceived as a delay between pushing edits to my README and seeing those changes reflected on the GitHub Pages site. It took me a while to notice this; so, for a while I worried that I was doing something wrong or missing something crucial. Is that delay normal? Is what I'm noticing not really a delay at all? Admittedly, I am not a "programmer" at the moment; so, I do not yet have the logic to confirm this myself. That said, we never simply are; rather, we are always becoming. Therefore, it would be more accurate to say that I am not yet as well as constantly becoming this notion of a programmer. 

With that in mind, I would like my work in this class to instill affect in others, though I am not yet sure what form that will take. Still, I wonder how much programming ability I will need to commit to such a creative aspiration. That said, I also wonder what kind of creation I am capable of given the limitations of my knowledge. Needless to say, I look forward to that process of discovery.

## 9/24/26

## 10/1/26
This week, my prototype-making process took a different direction. My previous set was primarily concerned with aesthetics, but this week’s prototypes are less visually striking. Though, what I find fascinating about this shift is that the meaning they carry feels much more palpable.

The "Slow Cancelation of the Future" prototype does exactly what its name would suggest, as defined by Mark Fisher. A rect shape gradually darkens over time, encroaching and growing larger as it envelops the screen until the "white" future is effectively canceled. I had a lot of fun trying to recreate this concept, but the most stimulating part was figuring out how to play audio in this prototype, something I carried over to my moon prototype. And I chose an audio recording of Mark Fisher himself giving a lecture on the concept (40 minutes long), which makes the experience particularly interesting. On one hand, Fisher is articulate and pleasant to listen to, but his concept is grounded in a kind of melancholic disposition. So, there's an odd parity in listening to him speak while the future in front of you is slowly canceled. Perhaps that feeling tells me I succeeded in representing the concept in some regard. One of my friends observing this prototype in class said something to the effect of, "here is something weirdly engaging about watching paint dry in this context" Maybe he's trying to point out this affect with such a statement.

My moon prototype also plays with slow movement, but it tries to provoke an inverse question. Every year, the moon gets farther away from us. Instead, I considered the inverse: what if the moon were to get closer to us every year? That idea fascinates me because, although the moon in the protoypse is moving closer at such a slow rate that it's barely perceptible, it has this uncanny effect of drawing nearer without us ever noticing. That said, that moment of surprise also allows for a strange sweet spot to consider: what would it be like to see the moon up close? In a morbid way (knowing that it would hit us), it must be kind of beautiful. I used audio from freesound.org to emphasize this sense of uncanny horror.

My last prototype was a mood switch; I wanted to give more meaning to the lightswitch concept used to explain conditions. It functions similarly, but instead of changing the background color, it relies on drawing and erasing shapes based on a mouse press. Effectively, the goal here was to illustrate that happiness takes effort in some way, shape, or form, which is why the mouse press is needed to maintain it. Without it, the yellow square equal on all sides becomes a sad blue, small and inequilateral rectangle. Upon testing it to see if it worked, I noticed that there was a body dysmorphia thing going on that I didn't necessarily intend: kind of cool that its there! Ultimately, I find that the interaction needs more gravity, as I recognize that in some ways it falls into a similar binary as the "press [f] to pay respects" interaction in *Call of Duty*. Maybe I can revisit it again in the future to rectify this.

All in all, I definitely understand variables much better now, and I have a solid grasp on conditionals. That said, I'm interested in seeing how far one can take conditionals. In class, we discussed how *Undertale* kind of overuses them, but I want to see what that would look like for myself, using my own thinking.

## 10/8/26
This week, my prototype-making process considered the use of conditionals along with events as I was watching the videos in tandem with making the prototypes themselves. Except for the masks prototype, my prototypes used images in order to express different aesthetic ideas. 

The most simple is scythe prototype, which used conditionals to recreate a damage hitmarker reaction in a video game. I drew the scythe in Corel Vector because I wanted to leverage that visual aspect of wielding a cool weapon. However, it only partially works, since the program relies on overlapping, and I've only had experience applying the methodology to ellipses rather than rectangles. That said, my method wasn't the most effective.

My next prototype only used image files, both made in Corel Vector: a modified dollar and a red rectangle the same size as the dollar with a foreboding message. This prototype is called "Critiquing Ideology" because I was inspired by Zizek's "A Pervert's Guide to the Critique of Ideology" (an amazing watch). In the opening, Zizek uses John Carpenter's "They Live" to explain that critiquing ideology first requires the subject to see through the ideology to perceive the real message. My prototype achieves this through mousewheel interaction: moving it up or down unveils the truth behind the ideological symbol (the dollar). I think it works quite well, but the film demonstrates this through a pair of sunglasses, I wonder if I can modify this in the future to somehow mask the "god" image behind a glasses png.

My last prototype uses key presses in order to demonstrate different affects. The basic ones are more surface level reactions ("s" for sad, "a" for angry, and "H" for sad) than they are affects, with the most interesting one being happy because in order to activate it, you have to write a capital H. This indicates that it takes more "effort" to be happy than it does it does to be sad or angry. Each key press changes the color of the face object corresponding to the affect. That said, where my affect concept stands out is with my ("w" for The Woven Web or "tww" as defined in the code). The idea is to demonstrate that songs are themselves affects. Affects are themselves cohesive feelings that feel abstract, but we can ultimately name. When the user presses "w" on the keyboard, the face "feels" like "The Woven Web". All this said, while I attributed "tww" to a color, I wanted to use an image in order to better illustrate the specificity of the affect. Though, I was unable to implement this because I don't know how to run audio and image files in the same project. I had to compromise and I opted to priotize the audio. Moreover, I wanted to layer a smiley face expression PNG over the face object (ellipse) in order to demonstrate the core concept of masks that I was going for, but because of this issue, I couldn't do so. In any case, the concept was to illustrate that society demands that we put a smile on our face no matter how we're feeling. In effect, that is our mask, and it is such whether we feel happy, sad, angry, or "The Woven Web"
