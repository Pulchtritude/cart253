## 10/1/26
This week, my prototype-making process took a different direction. My previous set was primarily concerned with aesthetics, but this week’s prototypes are less visually striking. Though, what I find fascinating about this shift is that the meaning they carry feels much more palpable.

The "Slow Cancelation of the Future" prototype does exactly what its name would suggest, as defined by Mark Fisher. A rect shape gradually darkens over time, encroaching and growing larger as it envelops the screen until the "white" future is effectively canceled. I had a lot of fun trying to recreate this concept, but the most stimulating part was figuring out how to play audio in this prototype, something I carried over to my moon prototype. And I chose an audio recording of Mark Fisher himself giving a lecture on the concept (40 minutes long), which makes the experience particularly interesting. On one hand, Fisher is articulate and pleasant to listen to, but his concept is grounded in a kind of melancholic disposition. So, there's an odd parity in listening to him speak while the future in front of you is slowly canceled. Perhaps that feeling tells me I succeeded in representing the concept in some regard. One of my friends observing this prototype in class said something to the effect of, "here is something weirdly engaging about watching paint dry in this context" Maybe he's trying to point out this affect with such a statement.

My moon prototype also plays with slow movement, but it tries to provoke an inverse question. Every year, the moon gets farther away from us. Instead, I considered the inverse: what if the moon were to get closer to us every year? That idea fascinates me because, although the moon in the protoypse is moving closer at such a slow rate that it's barely perceptible, it has this uncanny effect of drawing nearer without us ever noticing. That said, that moment of surprise also allows for a strange sweet spot to consider: what would it be like to see the moon up close? In a morbid way (knowing that it would hit us), it must be kind of beautiful. I used audio from freesound.org to emphasize this sense of uncanny horror.

My last prototype was a mood switch; I wanted to give more meaning to the lightswitch concept used to explain conditions. It functions similarly, but instead of changing the background color, it relies on drawing and erasing shapes based on a mouse press. Effectively, the goal here was to illustrate that happiness takes effort in some way, shape, or form, which is why the mouse press is needed to maintain it. Without it, the yellow square equal on all sides becomes a sad blue, small and inequilateral rectangle. Upon testing it to see if it worked, I noticed that there was a body dysmorphia thing going on that I didn't necessarily intend: kind of cool that its there! Ultimately, I find that the interaction needs more gravity, as I recognize that in some ways it falls into a similar binary as the "press [f] to pay respects" interaction in *Call of Duty*. Maybe I can revisit it again in the future to rectify this.

All in all, I definitely understand variables much better now, and I have a solid grasp on conditionals. That said, I'm interested in seeing how far one can take conditionals. In class, we discussed how *Undertale* kind of overuses them, but I want to see what that would look like for myself, using my own thinking.

## 10/8/26
This week, my prototype-making process considered the use of conditionals along with events as I was watching the videos in tandem with making the prototypes themselves. Except for the masks prototype, my prototypes used images in order to express different aesthetic ideas. 

The most simple is scythe prototype, which used conditionals to recreate a damage hitmarker reaction in a video game. I drew the scythe in Corel Vector because I wanted to leverage that visual aspect of wielding a cool weapon. However, it only partially works, since the program relies on overlapping, and I've only had experience applying the methodology to ellipses rather than rectangles. That said, my method wasn't the most effective.

My next prototype only used image files, both made in Corel Vector: a modified dollar and a red rectangle the same size as the dollar with a foreboding message. This prototype is called "Critiquing Ideology" because I was inspired by Zizek's "A Pervert's Guide to the Critique of Ideology" (an amazing watch). In the opening, Zizek uses John Carpenter's "They Live" to explain that critiquing ideology first requires the subject to see through the ideology to perceive the real message. My prototype achieves this through mousewheel interaction: moving it up or down unveils the truth behind the ideological symbol (the dollar). I think it works quite well, but the film demonstrates this through a pair of sunglasses, I wonder if I can modify this in the future to somehow mask the "god" image behind a glasses png.

My last prototype uses key presses in order to demonstrate different affects. The basic ones are more surface level reactions ("s" for sad, "a" for angry, and "H" for sad) than they are affects, with the most interesting one being happy because in order to activate it, you have to write a capital H. This indicates that it takes more "effort" to be happy than it does it does to be sad or angry. Each key press changes the color of the face object corresponding to the affect. That said, where my affect concept stands out is with my ("w" for The Woven Web or "tww" as defined in the code). The idea is to demonstrate that songs are themselves affects. Affects are themselves cohesive feelings that feel abstract, but we can ultimately name. When the user presses "w" on the keyboard, the face "feels" like "The Woven Web". All this said, while I attributed "tww" to a color, I wanted to use an image in order to better illustrate the specificity of the affect. Though, I was unable to implement this because I don't know how to run audio and image files in the same project. I had to compromise and I opted to priotize the audio. Moreover, I wanted to layer a smiley face expression PNG over the face object (ellipse) in order to demonstrate the core concept of masks that I was going for, but because of this issue, I couldn't do so. In any case, the concept was to illustrate that society demands that we put a smile on our face no matter how we're feeling. In effect, that is our mask, and it is such whether we feel happy, sad, angry, or "The Woven Web" 

## 10/20/26
This week, my set of prototypes using events were thought provoking for me in a lot of different ways. Im sensing a pattern here each week, this duality between substance and aestheticism; if I prototype something in the future that considers a balance between the two, I wonder what that would look like, and what the idea I would be exploring. Perhaps i've already achieved this in some capacity, but in any case, lets talk about my prototypes.

The first prototype was one that I attempted to start at the beginning of the reading week, and it hinged on this concept of a pink sunset. Perhaps I’m not paying close enough attention, but I haven’t noticed any striking pink sunsets in Montreal since I moved here for university, though they carry a very personal significance for me back at home. I grew up in an area about an hour commute from NYC with really bad light pollution, and that horrendous light pollution has a wonderful effect on sunsets in the summertime (ironic I know). I have many fond memories I associate with these sunsets. That said, the prototype is all about temporality and the nature of impermanence; the Japanese call this *mono no aware*. Pink sunsets, no matter how beautiful, are temporary by their very nature as "sunsets, reminding us that the present is always slipping away. You can press “p” to claw back the pink sunset to its peak in an attempt to hold onto it for a bit longer. This interaction begs the question: should we just let it go: the past, I mean? For the betterment of the “now” that’s slipping away, I say yes. Though, I show great sympathy and compassion for those who choose to press “p” to see the pink sunset once more.

The second prototype is super postmodern to me because there is nothing visual being drawn on the canvas. Instead, the blank canvas asks you to imagine, maybe even close your eyes if that helps. By using sound effects typical of first-person shooter games (apart from an actual gunshot because growing up in America, it's never a good idea to play a sound like that in an American pedgogical insitution) I employed events to simulate the auditory experience of playing an FPS game in order to evoke the corresponding visual experience in the player’s imagination. The thinking was to interrogate control frameworks in games—the idea that after playing a certain number of a certain type of game, our brains become conditioned to understand how to play regardless of game-specific tutorialization practices. I wanted to use the player’s assumptions to stimulate their imagination, and particularly, I want this program to provoke conversations between people. When you interact with the FPS control framework without any visuals but with all the auditory richness, what FPS game are you imagining you’re playing and why? It’s a wonderful question to ask to spark a conversation between two people who find value in thinking deeply about our anecdotal experience with games.

My third prototype emerged from a Ralph Waldo Emerson quote that randomly recurs as a thought in my brain. In his essay “The Poet”, Emerson makes the statement (I’m paraphrasing here) that the true poet only drinks water from a wooden bowl (I believe he was referencing Homer when staing this). That got me thinking: is Emerson saying that depending on your choice of drink, you “become” a different kind of poet? That got me thinking of my own experiences; whenever I write, I’m almost always sober, and that 5% when I’m not, I notice that there tends to be differences in the way I write and the content I attempt to explore. This prototype simply allows the player to choose a drink by pressing “w” (water), “b” (beer), or “mouseButton” (psychedelic tea, a secret option). For each option, I’ve selected poems/lyrics written by people from clearly different “consumptive” backgrounds: an Emerson poem for water, a Charles Baudelaire poem for beer (this guy drank a lot), and Maynard James Keenan of the band Tool for psychedelic tea. The latter of the three strikes me as humorous because it references something Keenan said in the audio documentary Maximum Tool. This was something along the lines of how Tool’s music makes you feel like you’re on drugs. Upon hearing this I thought to myself: "No, Keenan, I think it’s just all the drugs you’re taking that make you feel that way". A state of mind that is fully capable of producing lyrics for songs such as Lateralus, Vicarious, Third Eye and as used in this prototype: Rosetta Stoned. 

## 10/29/26
This week, I found myself struggling to come up with good ideas using functions, and I much prefer expressing interesting ideas over focusing on aesthetics. That said, one of my prototypes is purely aesthetic; all it does is use functions to organize the shared behavior between three ellipses (planets, in this case), each differentiated by a different fill color while maintaining their stroke. The program calls the random function for each planet to manipulate their size. That said, because of the stroke effect and the fact that this function is called in draw repeatedly, the planets' struggle for dominance leaves a trail that creates a cool visual effect. And if im being completely honest, I think the visual is better than the concept itself.

One of my other prototypes dealt with emotive states; it's something I keep coming back to because I'm trying to find different ways to explore it. Here, I drew three different ellipses differentiated by color and behavior: happiness, sadness, and anger. While all three are grouped by the function "face", not all three variables have the same data. For instance, happiness and sadness have a velocity data point, while anger just has a data point called rage that gets called in a random function. The program is trying to demonstrate that sadness and happiness move vertically: a state of happiness is built on elevation, while sadness is the opposite. Anger is not concerned with vertical behavior, so it is confined to the chaos of rapidly shifting horizontally.

My last prototype to me has the most interesting of the three ideas: the post-relationship behavior between two squares, Jim and Cat. Jim and Cat have recently just broken up, and went no contact; so, the two initially move linearly apart from one another. However, by using "wasd" (calling an events keyPressed function), the player can control the movements of jim, leaving a trail behind him. This is meant to demonstrate that when we end relationships or even friendships with other people, the process of moving on is rarely linear. Yet, the other party, as "the other" in relation to us as the subject (or Jim in this case), can only infer that "the other" is moving on at some of sort of recognizable pace. Cat may not be navigating her end of the breakup in a linear fashion, but to Jim (to us, the subject), she can only be visualized by some kind of pattern the subject can identify with. We understand that this pattern doesn't reflect her actual experience, but it's one of the only ways the subject can understand the other given the physical and emotional distance.
